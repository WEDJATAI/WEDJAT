# Certificate of Origin — Preferential and Non-Preferential Origin Proof

A **certificate of origin** (C/O, CO, or Declaration of Origin) attests that the product listed has met the criteria to be considered as **originating in a particular country**. It is generally prepared and completed by the exporter or manufacturer, may be subject to official certification by an authorized third party, and is often submitted to the customs authority of the importing country to justify the product's eligibility for entry and/or its entitlement to preferential treatment. Guidelines for issuance by chambers of commerce globally are issued by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC).

## Two families of certificates

| Family | Purpose | Issuance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Non-preferential** | Proves the country of origin for MFN treatment, trade-remedy cases, marking, statistical or contractual purposes | Usually not required unless specifically indicated (e.g., anti-dumping duty investigations in force); commonly chamber-certified |
| **Preferential** | Claims reduced/zero duty under a trade agreement's **rules of origin** | Mandatory in most cases to claim preference; issued under the specific agreement's form and issuer rules; small-value exemptions common |

## Country of origin vs export country

The country of origin does not refer to the country of the exporter but to the country where the product is obtained or produced — which may differ from the export country (goods made in B, exported by A's trader, imported to C). For goods not directly shipped, a transit country's competent authority may sometimes issue a **back-to-back certificate** based on an original.

## Who issues and verifies

- **Issuing bodies**: exporter/manufacturer self-certification where allowed (e.g. modern FTAs, NAFTA-style origin declarations); otherwise the certificate is signed by the exporter/manufacturer and **countersigned by a local issuing body — typically a chamber of commerce or a customs authority**. ICC-accredited chambers follow the ICC International Certificate of Origin Guidelines. For FTA goods the issuer may be a Ministry of Trade agency instead.
- **Verification**: in most agreements the verifying authority is the **customs authority of the importing country** — rules of origin must pass verification there for the goods to receive preferential treatment.
- The certification officer may require manufacturing-process documents or examine the premises; issuance incurs fees and lead time.

## Common preferential forms (examples from the referenced agreements)

- **EUR.1 movement certificate** — EU preferential trade agreements (with accompanying exporter declarations on invoices for some regimes).
- **A.TR. movement certificate** — EU–Turkey customs-union goods.
- **Form A / Form E and similar** — GSP and ASEAN-style agreements (issuer/authority prescribed per agreement annex).
- **FTA-specific certificates** — e.g. Chile–Thailand agreement prescribes Form of Certificate of Origin per its Annex 4.13 sections; USMCA uses certification-of-origin declarations.

## Timing and validity

- A certificate must be submitted within the validity period of the applicable rules of origin, normally **at the time of importation** to claim preference.
- Many agreements allow **retroactive issuance** and tariff refund where the claim was not filed at importation for justifiable reasons.

## Separation of obligations

Even if the goods actually originate in a country, the **importer's failure to submit** a certificate of origin (issued/obtained by the exporter) may bar the goods from preferential treatment — the importer presents the proof; the exporter is the one able to prove origin to the issuer.
