# Veterinary and Health Certificates for Animal Products and Food

**Veterinary certificates** are official documents issued by a licensed veterinarian or a government authority confirming that animal-origin goods meet the sanitary requirements of the importing country. They are the zoosanitary mirror of the phytosanitary certificate: where plants need an NPPO certificate, live animals, meat, dairy, fishery products, embryos/semens and other animal products need a **veterinary (health) certificate** to cross borders.

## What they certify

| Aspect | Typical content |
| --- | --- |
| Health status | Animals/products free from notifiable diseases listed by the destination |
| Origin & inspection | Slaughterhouse/processing plant approval numbers, ante/post-mortem inspection |
| Treatment | Processing (e.g. heat treatment) or vaccination/test results where required |
| Traceability | Identification of the consignment (marks, weights, containers) |
| Authority | Issuing government veterinarian's name, stamp and signature |

## Issuing authority

Certifications must be issued by a **government-appointed veterinarian** and comply with the guidelines set by the relevant organizations (the WTO SPS Agreement framework, the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) standards, and the destination country's veterinary authority). Issuers are the **competent authority of the exporting country**:

- **United States**: USDA APHIS Veterinary Services issues veterinary health certificates for animal-product exports; the FDA Center for Veterinary Medicine issues export certificates for animal drugs/feeds; other FDA centers issue food-export certificates (e.g. for seafood) per product jurisdiction.
- **EU**: the official veterinarian at the approved establishment, on EU model certificates set by Commission regulation for the product type.
- Elsewhere: the national veterinary service of the exporting country.

## Import control context

- Destination veterinary authorities inspect the certificate **and often the goods** at the border control post; failures (missing stamp, wrong model, temperature abuse in transit) cause re-export or destruction.
- Many countries additionally require **import permits** for animal products and quarantine for live animals.
- For food generally, health/sanitary certificates (and free-sale certificates) issued by the competent food authority accompany the commercial documents.
- Fishery and aquaculture products commonly need a health certificate under the destination's hygiene regime plus catch certificates where illegal-unregulated-unreported (IUU) fishing laws apply.

## Practice checklist

1. Confirm the destination's **model certificate** and language requirements before shipment.
2. Book the government veterinarian's inspection at the right stage (pre-shipment, at slaughter/processing).
3. Ensure establishment approval numbers appear on the certificate and packaging.
4. Match certificate data exactly to the invoice and packing list (marks, weights, packages).
5. Cold-chain products: plan the certificate timeline against expiry and reefer setpoints (see the Container types reference).
