# Bill of Lading — Functions, Types and Legal Role

The bill of lading (B/L or BOL) is the document issued by a carrier (or the carrier's agent) to acknowledge receipt of cargo for shipment. Although historically tied to carriage by sea, the term is used for other modes as well. Together with the insurance policy and the commercial invoice, it is one of the three crucial documents of international trade used to ensure exporters receive payment and importers receive the merchandise. Whereas a bill of lading is negotiable, a policy of insurance and an invoice are merely assignable.

## The three functions

A bill of lading is transferable and serves three main functions:

1. **Conclusive receipt** — an acknowledgement that the goods have been loaded (it identifies the nature, quantity, quality and leading marks of the goods).
2. **Evidence of the contract of carriage** — it contains, or evidences, the terms of the contract between shipper and carrier.
3. **Document of title** — it serves as a document of title to the goods, subject to the *nemo dat quod non habes* rule (a transferee cannot obtain better title than the transferor had).

The classic definition comes from *Coventry v Gladstone* (1867): a writing signed on behalf of the owner of the ship acknowledging receipt of the goods and undertaking to deliver them at the end of the voyage, subject to the conditions mentioned in the bill. In *Glyn Mills & Co. v East and West India Dock Co* (1882) the court recognized any document which "by mercantile law and usage ... is a symbol of the right of property in the goods".

## Parties on the bill

| Party | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Carrier | Issues the B/L; undertakes carriage and delivery |
| Shipper | Delivers the cargo; usually the exporter or its forwarder |
| Consignee | Named recipient (on straight bills) or determined by endorsement (order bills) |
| Notify party | Party to be notified on arrival (often the importer or its agent) |

## Governing liability regimes

Most shipments by sea are covered by the **Hague Rules**, the **Hague-Visby Rules** or the **Hamburg Rules**, which require the carrier to issue the shipper a bill of lading identifying the nature, quantity, quality and leading marks of the goods. The proposed **Rotterdam Rules** use the umbrella term "transport documents", of which bills of lading and sea waybills are examples. In the United Kingdom, the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1992 grants "all rights of suit under the contract of carriage" to the lawful holder of a bill of lading, or to the consignee under a sea waybill or a ship's delivery order.

## Negotiability and transfer

A standard-form bill of lading is transferable by endorsement (or by lawful transfer of possession). Order bills are made out "to order [of X]" and pass by endorsement; straight bills name a specific consignee and are transferred by delivery subject to the carrier's terms. Because the B/L is a document of title, presenting the original at destination is normally required for the carrier to release the cargo (see the Telex release and Arrival notice references).

## Issuance practice

- The carrier issues the B/L after the shipping instructions are submitted and the goods are loaded (typically signed by the master or the carrier's agent).
- **Originals**: traditionally issued in a set of three originals; presentation of any one duly endorsed original triggers delivery.
- **Copies (non-negotiable)**: marked "copy — not negotiable", used for record-keeping and customs filing.
- A **clean** B/L carries no adverse remarks on the condition of goods; a **claused/foul** B/L notes defects and is rejected under letters of credit (see the Documentary credit documents reference).

## Relationship to other documents

- The **sea waybill** is the non-negotiable alternative — no document of title function.
- The **booking confirmation** precedes it; the **arrival notice** and **delivery order** follow it at destination.
- Under documentary credits (UCP 600), the B/L is the key transport document banks examine.
